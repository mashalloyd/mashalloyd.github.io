Good morning from Tarifa, the southernmost point of Europe and the Iberian Peninsula and gateway to Africa. where in all my years in Spain I have never been and always wanted to come. 

We are now back in Spain after a wonderful adventure in Gibraltar, Ceuta and Northern Morocco (Tetouan, Chefchaouen and Tanjiers) as well as Tarifa and I have many stories to tell you this week. This trip has been a history and geography lesson in itself and we have soaked in the local culture wherever we have been. You won't read much about visits to museums or the most important local monuments, but you will read about our interactions with local people and their history which is what we most appreciate when we travel. Before I continue, let me tell you a bit about Morocco where we arrived last Saturday. The first time we ever came was in 1980. I have been back but just to Marrakesh in about 2005 and again in 2016. A local asked me if I thought it had changed. I replied yes and no. It has developed but many things remain the same. Morocco is still medieval in many ways with quite a lot of apparent poverty.  It has a population of around 37 million  and the main languages are Arabic and Amazigh with Moroccan Arabic (Darija) widely spoken as well as French and Spanish. I should add French is only really spoken by the more educated people. Islam, of course, is the official state religion with 99% being Sunni Muslims. The country is governed with an iron hand, I would say, by aging King Mohammad VI who holds all the power and the wealth. Nominal GDP is just 5.41 dollars per person, 79% less than the global average. The minimum wage is supposed to be  290 euros per month but as I have witnessed, often it is a lot less and impossible to live on.  So, of course, there is a massive submerged economy where people try to eke out a living. Medical care is not free and that is a huge problem in this country.   Pensions also are very low with about 70% of the private sector earning less than 183 euros per month. No wonder we saw so many people selling what they could at every corner in the Medina in Tetouan and other places. As to women's rights, women may have power at home but have many legal inequalities. They can't get a passport, travel abroad,  sign documents without their husband's permission and if they divorce they face social stigma and financial problems. As to single mothers, as we saw with Jadilla's case in Ceuta last week, unwed mothers are ostracised both by their families and by society and it is considered a crime. Then there is the problem of LGBT rights; they are non existent and a crime but of course it exists although in secret. Unemployment is high, especially for the youth and that is why many try to enter Spain illegally in search of a better world. Now back to our road trip.

Last Sunday we spent a whole day in Tetouan or rather in the Medina (old town). Tetouan was an ancient Berber and Roman settlement called Tamuda. In 1492 Andalusian  and Jewish refugees fled there after the Reconquista and when the Jews were expelled. The Jews who fled Spain were and are still called today, Sephardis. Many of them still talk what is known as Ladino today. Both groups influenced the culture and the architecture is still apparent today. From 1913 to 1956 Tetouan was the capital of the Spanish Protectorate which happened after France and Spain divided the country between them. Thus still today, many people, especially elderly men, speak perfect Spanish. Franco gave the region back to Morocco in 1956. People, maybe not aware of this history and mainly come to see the Medina which was named a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1977. The Medina is the best preserved one in the country and the most complete Andalusian Medina in the world. I read it was a living monument to Hispano-Moorish culture.

From the Royal Palace we explored the old Jewish Quarter (The Mellah and also called The Little Jerusalem)and went in search of the last Synagogue. That was the highlight of our stay in Tetouan. We walked into what is the last one of 16 to 18 that existed at the historical peak of the Jewish Quarter.  Called Isaac Ben Walid, there we met the last custodian, 85 year old León Bentolila, who I later learned is one of the most remarkable and deeply respected figures in the city. We got talking to this remarkable man who showed us the 3000 year old Torah Arc. He is proud of his Sephardic origin and is married to Sarah from Melilla from whom he got his Spanish nationality. Once an important wholesaler of dates and nuts to the local market vendors, he is the one who with his wife conserves the last Synagogue. He told us there are only 7 Jews left in the city and that there are no more services. For there to be a service there have to be 10 Jewish men. So sad. 

Then something surreal and sublime happened. My husband recited a passage from the Bible in Ancient Hebrew. It wasn't just any quote, it was the Protoevangelium, the very first gospel or first prophecy of the coming of Christ from Genesis 3:15. I was awe struck but Leon was not, but I imagine he was surprised. He assumed my husband had to have been a priest as all Christian priests learn Ancient Hebrew for seminary training. The Protoevangelium represents the moment the Christian faith traces the first promise of the Messiah right back to the first book of the Old Testarment. I had to take a photograph of both men, one a Christian, one a Jew, both wearing a Kippah and standing in front of the Torah Ark containing the 10 commandments.  Two men of immense intellectual and cultural calibre standing in that synagogue in Tetuouan will remain with me always. How proud I was of my husband in that moment. He just laughed it off saying he couldn't remember much. But, oh, he does, as he learned Ancient Hebrew, Ancient Greek and Latin over 60 years ago yet can still recite many passages from all three tongues. What a moment.

Eladio quoted Pope John Paul's witty reply when told this on his famous visit to Israel: "why put limits on Divine Providence?". With the sat nav on, we began our drive to Chefchaouen, more commonly known as Chaouen. the Blue City or Blue Pearl. To be honest I had never heard of it until a waitress mentioned it at the Parador in Ceuta. I looked it up and fell in love with it just from the pictures I saw. Otherwise we would have gone straight to Tangiers. I am glad we went there. It is the most picturesque town or city I have ever seen. 

It was founded in 1471 by Moulay Ali Ibn Rashid al-Alamias as a small fortress in the Rif mountains for defence against the Portuguese.  It is surrounded by the peaks of the Rif mountains. The name actually means "look at horns", the horns being the peaks of the mountain range. Like Tetouan, many "Moriscos" (those Muslims who had to convert  to Christianity during the Spanish Inquisition) and Jews (expelled from Spain in 1492) came here. Again like Tetouan they gave it its blend of Andalusian-Moroccan heritage.  Spanish rule came in 1920 when troops captured the city during the Rif war. Many locals still speak Spanish. Like Tetouun  Franco returned it to Morocco in 1956. It only transitioned into the Blue City in the 1930's. It is said the Jews inspired the colour but it soon caught on. Others say it was to keep the mosquitoes at bay and others to represent the sea. Whatever the reason, it is the blue washed streets and houses that attract so many tourists here and now I understand why.

Wow, Tanjiers, what a melting pot. It is the 4th largest city in Morocco after Casablanca, Rabat and Fez and has about 1.25 million inhabitants. It sits at the crossroads of the Mediterranean and the Atlantic at the narrowest point of the Strait of Gibraltar. Its location being so strategic made it prized as a strategic port by the Phoenicians. the Romans, Vandals and Byzantines. The Arab conquest in the 8th century brought Islam culture to the region. In the 15th to the 17th centuries it was under Portuguese and British control. The latter withdrew in 1684 and in the 18th century  it became Morocco's main diplomatic and foreign trade centre. For many people, it is its more recent history that makes it unique. The Tangier International Zone was created in 1923 because European colonial powers were desperate to control the Strait of Gibraltar. To avoid a war the Tanjier Statute was signed between France, Britain and Spain declaring the city as a neutral zone. Over time, another 6 countries were incorporated into this council of sorts. To keep everyone happy there were no customs duties, no income tax and total banking secrecy. Thus it became a tax haven, major smuggling capital and a zone where no single country could impose its jurisdiction. It became a pull for spies, millionaires, political refugees and avant garde writers. In WW2 it was a playground for international espionage. The Spanish dictator Franco briefly ruled it during WW2. After the war, the international Zone status was restored with the then USSR joining the council. The Zone was finished with in 1956 when Morocco declared its independence. That put an end to its unique status and it took decades for the city to reinvent itself. No longer a tax haven nor smuggler paradise, now it houses the biggest port in Africa and welcomes many tourists. But still, its past remains in many ways. It is still a melting pot and you hear Spanish, French and Moroccan spoken everywhere and even some English. From what I saw on our very first walk it is where old Morocco meets the new Morocco.

We had lunch at Dada in the heart of the Medina and it was ok, not the best - that was El Cielo - but fine. During our meal we had to sort out a little crisis at home. The Mini had been serviced after some emergency which meant Suzy didn't have  a car to go to work with. The bill was enormous (oh dear) and somehow we had to work out a way of paying it on our phones from Morocco so she could pick up the car. That sort of spoiled our meal but we managed it. From Dada we walked the 1.5km "home" to our refuge and haven in Tanjiers.

That was our last full day in the city I am glad we visited. It lived up to my expectations after having seen a travel programme (Madrileños por el Mundo) about it, one Olivia used to work for as a travel reporter. 

Friday came, the day of our departure. We didn't have to be at the port until 11 am for our 13h ferry to Tarifa, so I suggested we go to the big Carrefour to stock up on dates and baklava to take home, as finally, there we would be able to pay by card. 
Carrefour in Tangiers

It was quite obvious that the French supermarket was in a very rich area of the town, a part we hadn't seen before. In we went and also bought some fruit and much needed toothpaste. While there we went to a tobacconist to get Chesterfield which costs half of what it costs in Spain. We were allowed 2 cartons but I took back many more at the risk of having them confiscated.

From there we drove to the Port (Ville), the small one in town from where our ferry would depart to Tarifa, Spain. We had never done that journey and wondered what it would be like, how it worked and how to go through Moroccan and Spanish security and customs. For sure, it was much easier than leaving Ceuta. We parked inside the port, went to redeem our tickets and then were kindly sent to the front of the queue by the Moroccan port workers. We even had a chat with the boss, Jamal. We had 1.5h to kill and spent part of it taking photos of the Kasbah and Medina as seen from the port.
Last photos in Tangjiers - this one was taken from the port
And here is one of Eladio by the port.
By the port in Tanjiers on Friday
At 11.30 we went back to our car and began the whole process. Everything was new for us and another adventure. We love our adventures. We went through passport control, then the car control after which we had to park it. We didn't understand why until we saw a huge big scanner that scanned all cars, searching, I suppose, for drugs. We then went through a visual inspection of the car that included sniffer dogs but this time, at least, we didn't have to open our suitcases. Security was strict but not tough. Soon we were on board with our car and went inside. We looked for the part of the boat where Eladio would feel least seasick and then I went up on deck to watch the ferry leaving the port and admire the views of the city and the Strait of Gibraltar. The whole journey took just over 45 minutes, less than from Algeciras to Ceuta, as the distance is only 14.5km. Soon I had to remove my Moroccan sim card and revert to using my Spanish sim card which was pretty seamless. Soon too we were approaching Tarifa, the southernmost town in Spain and in Europe where I had never been and always wanted to visit. 
Approaching Tarifa

We were the second car off the boat. Once on land we had to go through passport control which was easy peasy as were arriving in the EU. Customs was easy too. A friendly Guardia Civil asked if we were carrying any tobacco or spirits. I lied and said I had two cartons, when I had 5 (hahaha) and he waved us on. 

It was good to be back in clean and well organised Spain and what a difference from Morocco which,  although interesting for tourists, is basically, poor, dirty and backward. That though is not the fault of its lovely people but of its despotic monarchy. Why I ask myself is Spain rich and the country next door poor? The answer is how it is governed. 

Our hotel, the lovely Hotel Convento was 850m from the port and we were there in a jiffy and greeted by the receptionist, Lola, who is a lovely person. By then it was about 4.30 as Spain is 2 hours ahead of Morocco (when the clocks change here it will be 1 hour ahead - Morocco, btw, never changes the clocks) so as soon as we had checked in, we went out in search of food. Lola recommended a great bar called "El Francés" in the old town and it was excellent. 

Bar El Francés in the old town of Tarifa
We were soon tucking into "patatas bravas" followed by oxtail stew for Eladio and meat balls in tomato for me -  best I have ever had. Dessert was a wonderful concoction of coconut and baked pineapple. We walked back to our hotel with full stomachs and loved our room. It is large, has a small balcony, a little sitting room with a TV and a huge bathroom. Best of all the bed is enormous and I found it comfortable. We didn't go out again, just enjoyed our time in that lovely hotel, El Convento. Lola told us it was originally a Franciscan Priory in the 18th century. Then it was a garrison for Napoleon's troops in the Peninsular War. It went on to become the town hall, a school and today it is a boutique hotel. We love the architecture and it is just a few hundred metres from the Gate of Jérez, leading into the old town.

That night we watched the news for the first time in nearly 2 weeks but nothing was new as, of course we have been following the news the whole time, if more scantily than usual. It was all Spanish politics to do with government corruption cases and the election campaign. I preferred to read up on the history of Tarifa and plan Saturday, the last full day of our road trip.

I slept a whole 6.5h hours that night and woke up at 6.49 am. I consider that  a good night's sleep. We had a great breakfast after which we went out into the sunshine to explore Tarifa where the thermometer reached 26 degrees.

Tarifa is the southernmost town in Europe and the gateway to Africa and Europe. Today it thrives on tourism going to  and from Morocco through the Strait of Gibraltar and is the world capital of sun surfers and has a bit of a hippy vibe. We didn't come her for the surfing but for the town's geography and history. On one side you have the Mediterranean Sea and on the other the Atlantic Ocean. It  has quite a history of its own. Like Tanjiers, it was populated and ruled by Phoenicians, Greeks and Carthaginians and then the Romans. Parts of the Iberian Peninsula came under Islamic rule in 711 until 1492, 8 centuries, as you probably know and that included Tarifa. Spain in 1492 was still an area of various kingdoms and didn't become the nation we know today until much later, after the War of Spanish Succession in the early 1700s. This is what Spain or  I should say, the Iberian Peninsula  looked like in 1492 by the way.
The Iberian Peninsula in 1492

I am transgressing, so back to Tarifa. The origin of the name comes from the Berber commander Tarif ibn Malik who led a raid here. During the Muslim conquest, Tarifa developed as a fortified medieval Medina but the town's main fortress was the castle built by Caliph Abd-ar-Rahman III in 960.  During the Christian Reconquest in the middle ages, King Sancho IV of Castile, reconquered Tarifa in 1292. The hero of the day was Guzmán el Bueno from León where Eladio is from. He refused to surrender the castle even when the Moors threatened and killed his son. Today the castle is known as el Castillo de Guzmán el Bueno and my husband was keen to visit it. And that we did. But first we went through what the Moroccans would call the Medina, the old town, through the Gate of Jeréz.

I am tiring you with the history of Tarifa and Spain so let me tell you about our walk up to the Isla de Las Palomas (Pigeon Island) where the exact southernmost point is. You can't actually visit the island which is fortified by the military but you can walk to it on the road that separates the Mediterranean Sea and the Atlantic Ocean. It was a beautiful sunny day and I would have loved to go on the beach at such an important geographical point but I enjoyed the walk and got some good photos. It was a very happy moment. The best one is the one below.
Approaching Pigeon Island where the exact southernmost point is located. The road divides the Mediterranean Sea and the Atlantic Ocean. 
There is a little beach at the end on the left and I heard a passerby say he had just seen some Moroccans who had swum to it. So many risk their lives to come here, it is heartbreaking and a big problem too for Spain. 

We then walked the ancient streets of the old town and soaked in the vibes of the streets with their lovely whitewashed houses, so beautifully kept with their pretty patios.

We loved the old town of Tarifa, so picturesque and full of atmosphere
I had always wanted to visit Tarifa and it lived up to my expectations.

The last important item on the agenda on the last day of our road trip was lunch in Tarifa. We chose La Pescadería on  Paseo de la Alameda, a popular street with locals.  Recommended to us by Lola from the hotel, even though we were not very hungry after our huge breakfast, we really liked the place and the food. All we could manage though was a prawn and mango salad followed by a plate of local fried squid (puntillas). Divine. With our heads full of history and geography, our stomachs full of good local food and feeling so happy with life, we ambled back to our hotel to rest.

The clocks went back one hour last night marking the end of Sunmer time. I always hate it when the clocks go back and I wish it hadn't happened at the end of our road trip.

All good things come to an end. Our two week adventure packed road trip of the south of Spain, including Gibraltar  (haha) and North of Morocco has been wonderful with no major mishaps. Today is Sunday and we are going home. It is a long way back, over 700km which you will read about in next week's far less exciting blog post.

Cheers now from Tarifa and thanks for reading my blog,

Masha.
